Lion's Mane Mushroom and the Gut: Early Research

By the Gut Save editorial team · Published 25 November 2026 · Educational content, not medical advice

Lion's mane (Hericium erinaceus) is a white, shaggy mushroom that looks a bit like a pom-pom. It's eaten as food in parts of Asia and has a long history in traditional practice. Most headlines focus on brain-health research, but scientists are also interested in what it does in the gut.

Why a mushroom might affect gut bacteria

Mushrooms contain polysaccharides — complex carbohydrates that human enzymes can't fully break down. Those reach the colon, where gut bacteria can ferment them. That makes mushroom fibers a natural candidate for influencing the microbiome.

What the research shows so far

A narrative review in PubMed Central gathered lion's mane research related to the digestive tract and found that much of it is preclinical, meaning it comes from cell and animal studies.

In humans, a small pilot study gave 13 healthy adults lion's mane powder for seven days and analyzed their gut bacteria. The researchers reported increased microbial diversity and changes in some bacteria that produce short-chain fatty acids. It's an interesting early signal, but a seven-day study of 13 people can't establish lasting effects.

The honest bottom line

  • Lion's mane is a promising, actively researched ingredient for gut health.
  • Human evidence is still limited and early.
  • It's generally eaten as a food, but people with mushroom allergies should avoid it.
